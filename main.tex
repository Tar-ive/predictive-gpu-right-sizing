\begin{abstract}
\noindent Online-inference GPU fleets are routinely over-reserved, and predicting the smallest safe GPU slice for each pod promises large savings. We show that this promise breaks in two places. Using a six-month production trace from Alibaba (294{,}465 pods, ten held-out clusters), we treat the safety margin as part of the method, label oracle results as such, and report seen-cluster and unseen-cluster performance together. Under these conditions, a gradient-boosted model fails to beat a per-workload history rule: it is more accurate in only 4 of 10 unseen clusters ($p = 0.81$), and the margin rule moves savings about five times more than the choice of model. We then extend evaluation beyond prediction, to hourly safety, recovery and reuse. At a 5\% risk budget, 21\% of reserved GPU-hours are reclaimable with oracle margins and 11\% with deployable ones, and a six-hour fallback removes 81\% of short pod-hours while keeping 95\% of the savings. But only 4\% of the GPU-hours freed become whole GPUs that a new job can use with enough CPU, when preemptible pods are counted as occupants. Replaying trace snapshots through a scheduler simulator, sizing pods to their hourly need frees 8--10 points of usable GPUs under each of five placement policies, and about 18 points if CPU is sized together with the GPU, while the choice among those policies changes the result by under a point. Right-sizing is limited by CPU, not by GPU prediction or placement policy.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Interactive AI services keep tail latency low by over-reserving accelerators, so a large share of reserved GPU capacity sits idle. A natural response is \emph{right-sizing}: predict, when a pod is admitted, the smallest GPU slice that meets its need, and return the rest to the pool. A natural first estimate of the opportunity is the share of reservations that could be reclaimed.

Two questions separate that estimate from something an operator can act on. First, \emph{does a learned model still help on a cluster it has never seen, when compared fairly with a simple baseline?} Second, \emph{what does a prediction do once it becomes a scheduling decision?} A slice that looks safe per pod can run short hour by hour, and capacity that is freed on paper may be impossible for another job to use.

The first question has a mature methodology in machine learning. \citet{gulrajani2021search} showed that careful, equal-conditions evaluation erased the apparent advantage of domain-generalization methods over empirical risk minimization, and argued that a method without a model-selection rule is incomplete. \citet{koh2021wilds} established the practice of reporting in-distribution and out-of-distribution performance together on real-world shifts. We adopt both, with GPU clusters as domains and a history rule as the strong baseline. Those benchmarks stop at predictive performance. We go further, because a right-sizing prediction has no value until a scheduler acts on it.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A cluster-shift evaluation protocol for right-sizing} with leave-one-cluster-out testing, three explicitly defined margin-selection rules including a disclaimed oracle, a seen-versus-unseen comparison, and a pre-registered go/no-go gate (\cref{sec:protocol}).
  \item \textbf{A strong-baseline result.} A per-workload history rule matches or beats learned models on unseen clusters, is about three times safer per pod-hour, and needs no training. The margin rule moves savings about five times more than the model does (\cref{sec:q1,sec:q4}).
  \item \textbf{A measured leakage audit.} A subtle history-feature leak inflated the learned model's deployable savings by 1.9 points and raised its cluster wins from 3 to 5 of 10 (\cref{sec:q3}).
  \item \textbf{A decision ladder} that evaluates the same policies at five levels, from predictive loss to reusable capacity. The preferred policy changes with the level, and only 3.8\% of freed GPU-hours are reusable under today's placement when preemptible pods count as occupants (\cref{sec:q4,sec:q5}).
  \item \textbf{A scheduler-simulator study.} Replaying 1{,}260 trace snapshots through the FGD simulator shows that pod sizing and CPU co-sizing, not the placement policy, decide how much freed capacity can be reused (\cref{sec:q6}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Problem Setting}
\label{sec:setting}

A pod $i$ is admitted with features $x_i$ and requests $r_i$ GPUs for $h_i$ hours. Its \emph{minimum viable fraction} is
\begin{equation}
y_i = \min\bigl(1,\ \max\bigl(\mathrm{P90}(\text{compute share}),\ \mathrm{P90}(\text{memory share})\bigr)\bigr),
\label{eq:mvf}
\end{equation}
computed over its observed hours. A sizing policy assigns a fraction $s_i$ from the slice sizes observed in the trace, a grid of 27 sizes that jumps from 0.5 to 0.7 to 1.0 GPU. We report the \emph{under-provisioning rate} and the \emph{reclaimed share} of reserved GPU-hours:
\begin{equation}
U = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \mathbf{1}[s_i < y_i], \qquad R = \frac{\sum_i h_i r_i (1 - s_i)}{\sum_i h_i r_i}.
\label{eq:ur}
\end{equation}
The goal is to maximize $R$ subject to $U \le 5\%$. Each cluster is a domain: clusters differ in hardware, workload mix and failure rate, and a policy is deployed on clusters that were not available when it was built.

\paragraph{Data.} We use the Alibaba Serverless Infrastructure (ASI) trace released with OSDI~'26~\citep{li2026asi,alibaba2026trace}, which covers six months and up to 155{,}410 GPUs. Scope is online-inference recommendation and GenAI pods with a GPU request. D1 (right-sizing) holds 294{,}465 whole-GPU pods observed for at least 24 hours. D2 (placement) holds 2{,}826{,}036 pods with a readiness label and a 46\% failure rate. Between 61\% and 68\% of pods have no workload history.

\paragraph{Models.} The baseline \textbf{A1} is the per-workload 90th percentile of past need over the last 7 days, using only pods finished before admission, with a model-type $\times$ GPU-type fallback. \textbf{A2} is regularized linear quantile regression and \textbf{A3} is XGBoost quantile regression with monotone constraints on history features, both at $\tau = 0.90$. Each family received the same budget of three configurations chosen on validation data only. For placement risk, \textbf{B1}, \textbf{B2} and \textbf{B3} are logistic regression, random forest and LightGBM classifiers of failure to become ready.

\paragraph{Splits and held-out clusters.} Days 14--129 are used for training, 130--157 for validation and calibration, and 158--183 for testing. For each of the 10 clusters with at least 9{,}000 pods, models are trained on the other clusters' training days and scored on the held-out cluster's test days.

\paragraph{Margin selection is part of the method.} A prediction becomes a slice through a safety margin and rounding up to the grid, so an evaluation that does not say how the margin was chosen is incomplete, in the same sense that \citet{gulrajani2021search} apply to hyperparameters. We report three rules:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Cross-cluster conformal (deployable).} A split-conformal margin~\citep{romano2019cqr,angelopoulos2021gentle} per model type $\times$ cold/warm cell, fitted on other clusters and targeting 95\% coverage. It uses no information from the test cluster.
  \item \textbf{Per-cluster recalibration.} The margin is re-estimated on part of the held-out cluster and scored on its remaining test days. This assumes a labelled sample from the new cluster.
  \item \textbf{Matched risk (oracle).} The margin is tuned so that each model sits at exactly $U = 5\%$ in each test cluster. \emph{This uses test labels and is not a valid deployment estimate.} We report it because it equalizes risk across models and bounds the opportunity.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Pre-registered gate.} A3 passes only if it beats A1 on pinball loss in at least 8 of 10 clusters, with Wilcoxon $p \le 0.05$, and reclaims more while keeping $U$ near 5\%. Later analyses are labelled exploratory, or as having a decision rule set before running, and H11 and H12 were tested on days already used.

\paragraph{The decision ladder.} Predictive metrics are the first of five levels at which the same policies are compared:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{L\arabic*.},start=0,leftmargin=2.2em]
  \item \emph{Prediction:} pinball loss.
  \item \emph{Per-pod decision:} $U$ and $R$ after margin and rounding.
  \item \emph{Hourly safety:} the slice is compared with actual compute and memory in every hour the pod ran.
  \item \emph{Recovery policy:} the pod returns to its full request after $k$ consecutive short hours.
  \item \emph{Reusable capacity:} the freed slice must complete a whole free GPU on that server, with enough free CPU for a new job.
\end{enumerate}

\finding{The learned model's value is confined to pods without history.} Almost all reclaimable capacity is in recommendation pods with workload history (33--43\% on seen clusters), while GenAI pods have a median minimum viable fraction of 0.983. It is the strongest candidate policy, but its advantage does not survive multiple-testing correction.

\subsection{Cluster shift removes the learned model's advantage}
\label{sec:q2}

\finding{The learned model's edge disappears on new clusters.} A3's pinball loss rises from 0.0237 on seen clusters to 0.0259 on unseen clusters, while A1 is unchanged (0.0249 and 0.0247). In three rolling temporal windows on seen clusters at matched risk, A3 reclaims 20.5--23.6\% against 13.9--17.4\% for A1. The learned model's disadvantage is a transfer problem, not a modelling failure.

\finding{Coverage does not transfer, and recalibration does not repair it.} A3's coverage is 92.2\% on unseen clusters against a 95\% target, and its worst single-cluster $U$ reaches 7.3\%, compared with 1.9\% for A1 in the same re-run. Per-cluster recalibration raises reclaim by 3.7--5.6 points but leaves coverage at about 93\%. It mainly loosens margins that were too conservative.

\finding{Risk estimates transfer worse than sizing does.} \Cref{tab:modelB} shows that every placement model loses discrimination on unseen clusters and roughly doubles its calibration error (\cref{fig:modelb}). Failure rates range from 35\% to 67\% by cluster, so a model trained elsewhere cannot know a new cluster's base rate. New features improved seen clusters (ROC-AUC 0.79, ECE 0.030) but not unseen ones significantly.

\finding{The leak favoured the learned model.} A1 is unaffected because it already used the strict rule. Removing the leak lowers A3 by 0.3 points at the oracle and by 1.9 points at deployable margins (14.6\% to 12.7\%), and cuts its wins from 5 to 3 of 10 clusters (\cref{tab:e9}). \finding{The baseline strengthens as the target becomes stricter.} Sizing each pod for its P99 or busiest hour reduces reclaim by about a third, and A3 falls behind A1 under both labels. The defensible range is therefore about 21\% for a P90 target and 13--14\% when memory must be safe at its peak.

\subsection{Hourly risk is concentrated, and a simple fallback removes most of it}

\finding{Two different problems hide in the shortfall rate.} The typical shortfall lasts 2 hours and is about 0.1 GPU. But about 1\% of pods stay short for a day or more and account for 66\% of A1's short hours and 73\% of A3's. The longest last 627--648 hours, so some pods were mis-sized from the start.

\finding{A simple recovery policy solves most of the safety problem.} Restoring the full request after six consecutive short hours cuts A1's short pod-hours by 81\% while keeping 95\% of the freed GPU-hours, satisfying a rule set before running. The rule holds pooled but not in every cluster (the cut is 20\% in \texttt{4d6f}). Early-life usage is a poor guide: raising a slice to the peak of the first 6 or 24 hours cut shortfalls by only 8--34\%.

\subsection{Most freed capacity cannot be reused}
\label{sec:q5}

We replayed each hour of days 158--184 with pods fixed on their actual servers, across 37{,}832 servers in the 10 unseen clusters. Test pods were shrunk to their predicted slices and all other pods kept their requests. A freed slice counts as usable only if it completes a whole free GPU on that server and the server has enough free CPU for a new job needing the cluster's median CPU per GPU.

\finding{Reclaimable is not reusable.} Only 3.8\% of freed GPU-hours are usable, against a threshold of 50\% set before running (\cref{tab:chain}). The hypothesis fails even when CPU is ignored (30\%). Without GPU sharing, usable capacity falls to 1.2\%.

\finding{Two limits act together.} The first is \emph{fractional residue}: a pod cut from 1.0 to 0.5 GPU frees half a GPU, and only about 30\% of freed slices add up to whole free GPUs on the same server. The second is \emph{insufficient CPU}, the condition FGD calls ``stranded''~\citep{weng2023fgd}: shrinking a pod releases GPU but not CPU. Clusters \texttt{da8d} and \texttt{4d6f} produced 39\% of freed GPU-hours but only about 6\% of the usable gain, because only 11--17\% of their free whole GPUs had enough CPU even before shrinking. This agrees with ASI's report that CPU shortage is a leading cause of unallocatable GPUs~\citep{li2026asi}.

\finding{The replay isolates what placement allows today.} Pods stay on their servers, only test pods are shrunk (freed GPU-hours are 1.2\% of capacity), and a new job needs the cluster's median CPU. The 3.8\% therefore measures reuse under current placement, with every pod, including preemptible and best-effort pods, counted as holding its capacity. Fleet-wide shrinking, CPU co-sizing and repacking are the levers that can raise it, and \cref{sec:q6} tests them in a simulator.

\finding{The trace cannot validate placement risk for shrunk pods.} Shrinking pods and re-scoring them with Model B reclaims 11.4\% of long-running pods' GPU-hours, but restricted to slice sizes Model B has seen, reclaim collapses to 1.8\%. The trace barely contains the fractional allocations a right-sizing policy would create, so the joint policy is a what-if.

\subsection{Repacking in a scheduler simulator (exploratory)}
\label{sec:q6}

The capacity replay keeps every pod on its server. To ask what a scheduler could do with freed capacity, we replayed trace snapshots through the open-source FGD simulator~\citep{weng2023fgd}, which implements FGD and five baseline placement policies. Before using ASI data, we confirmed that our build reproduces the authors' published allocation results exactly for every deterministic policy. We raised the simulator's limit of 8 GPUs per node to 16, because ASI has 16-GPU servers, and the patched build still matches.

\paragraph{Setup.} A snapshot is the set of pods running at the start of an hour (present in that hour and the one before) on one top-of-rack switch group (ASW) of a held-out cluster. We sampled three ASWs with at least 8 servers per cluster and 42 hours from days 158--183: 24 random hours plus two from each cluster's top decile of GPU allocation. This gives 1{,}260 snapshots averaging 46 servers, 427 GPUs and 500 placed pods. Only high-priority (HP) pods are placed, because they hold guaranteed resources: with HP pods alone, fewer than 0.5\% of servers exceed their CPU or GPU capacity, while adding preemptible low-priority and best-effort pods puts 46\% of servers over CPU capacity. Pods keep their GPU type. Each run places a snapshot's pods from empty in a seeded random order, since the trace has no arrival order within an hour. The metric is leftover whole free GPUs that still have 8 free CPU cores each, as a share of all GPUs.

\paragraph{Sizing variants.} Pods keep their original requests; or test pods with an A1 prediction get A1's slice (about 31 of 500 HP pods per snapshot); or every HP online-inference GPU pod gets its average need in that hour, snapped up to the slice grid. The last is an \emph{hourly-need ceiling}, not a deployable policy: it uses the hour's own utilization, and averages rather than peaks. Each variant is also run with the pod's CPU request scaled by the same fraction (\emph{CPU co-sizing}). Intervals are 95\% bootstraps that resample clusters.

\finding{The simulator fits the trace.} With original requests, FGD leaves only 0.03 pods per snapshot unplaced and leaves 22.0\% of GPUs usable, against 21.1\% in the trace's own placement (\cref{tab:emulator}). Total free capacity is equal by construction, so this tests fragmentation and feasibility rather than allocation. The causes of unusable capacity agree with ASI's fragmentation analysis~\citep{li2026asi} in two respects: fractional residue is negligible (0.4--2.2\% of free GPU), and insufficient CPU dominates for the 1G8C and 8G128C job profiles. They differ in one: for the 4-GPU profiles, insufficient CPU slightly exceeds multi-GPU shortfall.

\finding{How capacity is counted matters more than any policy.} Counting every pod as an occupant, as the capacity replay does, leaves 2.8\% of GPUs usable; counting only HP pods leaves 21.1\%. Most of the difference is capacity held by preemptible and best-effort pods, which ASI already uses to harvest idle reservations~\citep{li2026asi}. The 3.8\% in \cref{sec:q5} therefore measures reuse when freed capacity competes with those pods. It is a lower bound, not the reusable share for guaranteed work. We have not re-run the capacity replay with HP-only accounting.

\finding{A1's gain is real but small.} A1 adds 0.6 points of usable GPUs, because it resizes only the test pods it has a prediction for, about 6\% of HP pods.

\finding{Without CPU co-sizing, much of the freed GPU is stranded.} The hourly-need ceiling frees 15.7 points of whole GPUs, but only 9.5 points are usable with 8 cores each. Scaling CPU with the slice makes almost all of it usable ($+18.3$ points). This is the simulator counterpart of the insufficient-CPU limit in \cref{sec:q5}. Spike hours give the same results (A1 $+0.6$, ceiling $+9.2$, ceiling with CPU co-sizing $+17.5$).

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Evaluation under distribution shift.} \citet{gulrajani2021search} and \citet{koh2021wilds} are our methodological references (\cref{tab:mapping}). They evaluate predictive performance, while our ladder continues to decision outcomes on a domain where the prediction is consumed by a scheduler. Conformal methods~\citep{romano2019cqr,angelopoulos2021gentle} provide our margins, and our results show the exchangeability assumption failing across clusters.

\paragraph{Workload prediction and autoscaling.} Autopilot~\citep{rzadca2020autopilot} and Resource Central~\citep{cortez2017resource} show that history-based prediction works at scale. Helios~\citep{hu2021helios} and ASI~\citep{li2026asi} characterize GPU clusters. Our finding that a history rule is hard to beat under shift is consistent with those deployments.

\paragraph{GPU fragmentation and sharing.} FGD~\citep{weng2023fgd} targets fragmentation in GPU-sharing clusters, and ASI reports that fractional fragmentation is negligible in its fleet because GPU sharing is rarely used. Right-sizing would create those fractional allocations, which is why residue appears in our replay. We use FGD's open-source simulator for the repacking study in \cref{sec:q6}. Interference-aware systems~\citep{strati2024orion,xu2022igniter,shubha2024usher} study the safety of sharing, which a trace cannot observe.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Replication.} DomainBed repeats its full sweeps three times with new random choices, and WILDS uses at least three replicates. We report one pipeline run per configuration, with paired tests across clusters.
  \item \textbf{ID comparison is not test-to-test.} WILDS recommends measuring in-distribution performance on the test distribution. Our seen-cluster results use future days across clusters rather than the 10 held-out clusters themselves.
  \item \textbf{No latency data.} $U$ and short pod-hours are proxies, and the trace has no request-latency field. Usage was recorded with the full GPU available, so a shortfall's true effect is not observed.
  \item \textbf{Hourly resolution and P90 replays.} Sub-hour bursts are invisible, and the replays used slices built on the P90 label, so they are probably slightly optimistic for a memory-peak-safe policy.
  \item \textbf{Test days have been reused.} H5--H10 are exploratory, and H11 and H12 had rules set before running on days already used. No later days remain for a fresh holdout.
  \item \textbf{Simulator scope.} The simulator models CPU and GPU but not memory, places static snapshots without arrivals or departures, and keeps pods within their ASW. The hourly-need ceiling uses hourly averages. Random placement is not bit-for-bit reproducible, because the simulator draws random numbers during parallel scoring.
  \item \textbf{One operator.} All results come from one six-month trace with 10 usable held-out clusters.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

On clusters it has not seen, a learned right-sizing model fails to beat a per-workload history rule, and the safety margin moves savings about five times more than the model does. A fifth of reserved GPU-hours is over-reserved at a 5\% risk budget, and a six-hour fallback removes most of the remaining hourly risk. The decisive result is at the end of the ladder: only 3.8\% of freed GPU-hours can serve another job under today's placement, when preemptible pods count as occupants. A scheduler simulator shows that how pods are sized, and whether their CPU shrinks with them, matters far more than which placement policy repacks them. More investment in GPU prediction targets the wrong constraint. The path to real savings is to size GPU and CPU together.

\section{Preliminary: Incumbent Scheduler Rules}
\label{app:schedulers}

An unmerged analysis replays sizing rules recovered from the release binaries of six open-source GPU schedulers (VPA, kube-scheduler, KAI, HAMi, Volcano, Koordinator) on 22 days of the trace. All six size GPUs from what the user declares, so the deployed incumbent reclaims nothing, and a fixed factor such as 85\% snaps back to a whole GPU on the slice grid. On clusters seen in training, 90--95\% of each policy's reclaim survives cluster-level packing when server and CPU limits are ignored. Together with \cref{sec:q5}, this suggests that server and CPU limits, not packing itself, account for much of the loss. These numbers are preliminary: the window is short, pods still running at its end have truncated labels, the recovered constants have not been cross-checked against source, and VPA has not been evaluated on unseen clusters. We therefore do not report its model comparisons here.
