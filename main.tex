\begin{abstract}
\noindent Online-inference GPU fleets are routinely over-reserved, and predicting the smallest safe GPU slice for each pod promises large savings. We show that this promise breaks in two places. Using a six-month production trace from Alibaba (294{,}465 pods, ten held-out clusters), we treat the safety margin as part of the method, label oracle results as such, and report seen-cluster and unseen-cluster performance together. Under these conditions, a gradient-boosted model fails to beat a per-workload history rule: it is more accurate in only 4 of 10 unseen clusters ($p = 0.81$), and the margin rule moves savings about five times more than the choice of model. We then extend evaluation beyond prediction, to hourly safety, recovery and reuse. At a 5\% risk budget, 21\% of reserved GPU-hours are reclaimable with oracle margins and 11\% with deployable ones, and a six-hour fallback removes 81\% of short pod-hours while keeping 95\% of the savings. But only 4\% of the GPU-hours freed become whole GPUs that a new job can use with enough CPU, when preemptible pods are counted as occupants. Replaying trace snapshots through a scheduler simulator, sizing pods to their hourly need frees 8--10 points of usable GPUs under each of five placement policies, and about 18 points if CPU is sized together with the GPU, while the choice among those policies changes the result by under a point. Right-sizing is limited by CPU, not by GPU prediction or placement policy.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Interactive AI services keep tail latency low by over-reserving accelerators, so a large share of reserved GPU capacity sits idle. A natural response is \emph{right-sizing}: predict, when a pod is admitted, the smallest GPU slice that meets its need, and return the rest to the pool. A natural first estimate of the opportunity is the share of reservations that could be reclaimed. Over-reservation is only part of the waste. How jobs are scheduled decides whether reclaimed capacity can be used at all, and current practice handles this poorly: incumbent schedulers size GPUs from what users declare (\cref{app:schedulers}), and a freed GPU is wasted when its server has no CPU left for a new job (\cref{sec:q5,sec:q6}).

Two questions separate that estimate from something an operator can act on. First, \emph{does a learned model still help on a cluster it has never seen, when compared fairly with a simple baseline?} Second, \emph{what does a prediction do once it becomes a scheduling decision?} A slice that looks safe per pod can still run short hour by hour, because it is sized to a typical hour (the 90th percentile of need), not the busiest one. Capacity freed on paper may also be impossible for another job to use, for three reasons. Slices come only in fixed sizes, so a pod cut from 1.0 to 0.5 GPU leaves half a GPU that no whole-GPU job can take. Sharing a GPU between pods needs isolation that most clusters do not enforce, so ASI rarely shares GPUs~\citep{li2026asi}. And shrinking a pod's GPU does not shrink its CPU, so a freed GPU can sit on a server with no cores left for a new job.

The first question has a mature methodology in machine learning. \citet{gulrajani2021search} showed that careful, equal-conditions evaluation erased the apparent advantage of domain-generalization methods over empirical risk minimization, and argued that a method without a model-selection rule is incomplete. \citet{koh2021wilds} established the practice of reporting in-distribution and out-of-distribution performance together on real-world shifts. We adopt both, with GPU clusters as domains and a history rule as the strong baseline. Those benchmarks stop at predictive performance. We go further, because a right-sizing prediction has no value until a scheduler acts on it.

\paragraph{Contributions.} We contribute \textbf{Reclaim-to-Reuse (R2R)}, an evaluation framework that follows a right-sizing policy from prediction to usable capacity, and apply it to the ASI trace. R2R has three parts, each specified so it can be rerun on another trace (\cref{sec:framework}):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Shift Test.} Train on some clusters and test on a held-out one; choose safety margins by one of three declared rules (deployable conformal, per-cluster recalibration, or an oracle marked as such); and pass or fail a learned model against a history-rule baseline with a gate fixed before testing.
  \item \textbf{Leak Check.} Build history features only from pods that finished before the current pod was admitted, and report how much a history-feature leak moves the result when this rule is relaxed.
  \item \textbf{Reuse Ladder.} Score the same policy at each rung: prediction loss, per-pod risk and reclaim, hourly shortfall, a recovery fallback, and whole GPUs a new job can use, first in place and then after repacking in a scheduler simulator.
\end{itemize}
Applied to ASI, R2R shows where right-sizing pays and what it takes. A per-workload history rule, deployable without training, matches a learned model on unseen clusters, and choosing the safety margin matters five times more than choosing the model. With a six-hour fallback, the rule keeps 95\% of its savings while removing 81\% of short pod-hours. R2R also locates the next lever: in a scheduler simulator, sizing CPU together with GPU raises usable whole GPUs by up to 18 points, whatever the placement policy. Code, configurations and summary results are released (\cref{app:repro}).

\section{Background and Motivation}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Who over-reserves GPUs, and why}
\label{sec:bg-overres}

Large operators run online inference for many internal teams on a shared GPU fleet. Each team deploys its model as pods and declares, at deployment, how many GPUs (or what fraction of one) and how many CPU cores each pod needs. The scheduler reserves exactly what is declared, for as long as the pod runs, whether or not the pod uses it. Teams have every reason to declare generously: an under-sized inference pod misses its latency target or runs out of GPU memory and crashes, while an over-sized one costs the team nothing directly. The result is a fleet that is fully reserved but lightly used. ASI, the Alibaba fleet whose trace we use, reports a median SM utilization of 6\% for online inference and an allocation ratio of 68\% for high-priority work before its SpotGPU mechanism lends idle reservations to preemptible jobs~\citep{li2026asi}. The operator bears the cost: reserved but idle GPUs cannot be given to new services, so new services wait or the fleet grows.

\subsection{Why right-sizing is hard}
\label{sec:bg-hard}

The obvious fix is for the platform, not the team, to choose each pod's size. Four facts make that hard:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Need must be predicted at admission.} The size is fixed when the pod starts, before its own usage is seen, and 61--68\% of pods have no workload history to learn from.
  \item \textbf{Need changes hour by hour.} A size that covers a typical hour can fall short in a busy one, and a shortfall in GPU memory can crash the pod.
  \item \textbf{Clusters differ.} Hardware, workload mix and failure rates vary by cluster, so a model trained on one cluster may not hold on another.
  \item \textbf{Freed capacity is not automatically usable.} A slice freed on one server is useful only if, with other free capacity on that server, it forms a GPU that another job can take, with CPU beside it.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Right-sizing: need, risk and reclaim}
\label{sec:setting}

A pod $i$ is admitted with features $x_i$ and requests $r_i$ GPUs for $h_i$ hours. Its \emph{minimum viable fraction} is
\begin{equation}
y_i = \min\bigl(1,\ \max\bigl(\mathrm{P90}(\text{compute share}),\ \mathrm{P90}(\text{memory share})\bigr)\bigr),
\label{eq:mvf}
\end{equation}
computed over its observed hours. A sizing policy assigns a fraction $s_i$ from the slice sizes observed in the trace, a grid of 27 sizes that jumps from 0.5 to 0.7 to 1.0 GPU. We report the \emph{under-provisioning rate} and the \emph{reclaimed share} of reserved GPU-hours:
\begin{equation}
U = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \mathbf{1}[s_i < y_i], \qquad R = \frac{\sum_i h_i r_i (1 - s_i)}{\sum_i h_i r_i}.
\label{eq:ur}
\end{equation}
The goal is to maximize $R$ subject to $U \le 5\%$. Each cluster is a domain: clusters differ in hardware, workload mix and failure rate, and a policy is deployed on clusters that were not available when it was built.

\subsection{Fragmentation and CPU stranding}
\label{sec:bg-frag}

A freed slice is only useful if another job can be placed on it. FGD~\citep{weng2023fgd} targets fragmentation in GPU-sharing clusters and calls GPUs that lack CPU ``stranded''. ASI reports that fractional fragmentation is negligible in its fleet because GPU sharing is rarely used, and that CPU shortage is a leading cause of unallocatable GPUs~\citep{li2026asi}. Right-sizing would create the fractional allocations that the fleet avoids today, and it shrinks GPU without releasing CPU. Both effects decide how much reclaimed capacity becomes reusable.

\section{The Reclaim-to-Reuse Framework}
\label{sec:framework}

\subsection{Shift Test: held-out clusters, declared margins and a fixed gate}
\label{sec:protocol}

\paragraph{Held-out clusters.} Models are trained on some clusters and scored on a cluster they have never seen (leave-one-cluster-out), and seen-cluster results are reported beside unseen ones.

\paragraph{Margin selection is part of the method.} A prediction becomes a slice through a safety margin and rounding up to the grid, so an evaluation that does not say how the margin was chosen is incomplete, in the same sense that \citet{gulrajani2021search} apply to hyperparameters. We report three rules:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Cross-cluster conformal (deployable).} A split-conformal margin~\citep{romano2019cqr,angelopoulos2021gentle} per model type $\times$ cold/warm cell, fitted on other clusters and targeting 95\% coverage. It uses no information from the test cluster.
  \item \textbf{Per-cluster recalibration.} The margin is re-estimated on part of the held-out cluster and scored on its remaining test days. This assumes a labelled sample from the new cluster.
  \item \textbf{Matched risk (oracle).} The margin is tuned so that each model sits at exactly $U = 5\%$ in each test cluster. \emph{This uses test labels and is not a valid deployment estimate.} We report it because it equalizes risk across models and bounds the opportunity.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Pre-registered gate.} A3 passes only if it beats A1 on pinball loss in at least 8 of 10 clusters, with Wilcoxon $p \le 0.05$, and reclaims more while keeping $U$ near 5\%. Later analyses are labelled exploratory, or as having a decision rule set before running, and H11 and H12 were tested on days already used.

\subsection{Leak Check: history from finished pods only}
\label{sec:leakcheck}

History features summarize a workload's past need. A sibling pod admitted on an earlier day may still be running, so its label can include hours after the current pod's admission. The Leak Check builds history only from pods \emph{finished} before admission and reports how much each result moves when the rule is relaxed to pods \emph{admitted} before it.

\subsection{Reuse Ladder: from prediction to usable capacity}
\label{sec:ladder}

Predictive metrics are the first of five rungs at which the same policies are compared:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{L\arabic*.},start=0,leftmargin=2.2em]
  \item \emph{Prediction:} pinball loss.
  \item \emph{Per-pod decision:} $U$ and $R$ after margin and rounding.
  \item \emph{Hourly safety:} the slice is compared with actual compute and memory in every hour the pod ran.
  \item \emph{Recovery policy:} the pod returns to its full request after $k$ consecutive short hours.
  \item \emph{Reusable capacity:} the freed slice must complete a whole free GPU on that server, with enough free CPU for a new job.
\end{enumerate}
L4 is measured twice: in place, with every pod kept on its server, and after repacking in a scheduler simulator (\cref{sec:simmethod}).

\subsection{Repacking in a scheduler simulator}
\label{sec:simmethod}

To ask what a scheduler could do with freed capacity, R2R replays trace snapshots through the open-source FGD simulator~\citep{weng2023fgd}, which implements FGD and five baseline placement policies. A snapshot is the set of pods running at the start of an hour (present in that hour and the one before) on one top-of-rack switch group (ASW). Only high-priority (HP) pods are placed, because they hold guaranteed resources, and pods keep their GPU type. Each run places a snapshot's pods from empty in a seeded random order, since the trace has no arrival order within an hour. The metric is leftover whole free GPUs that still have 8 free CPU cores each, as a share of all GPUs. Because the simulator places the same pods on the same servers as the trace, it also validates the framework: with original requests it must reproduce the trace's leftover capacity before any sizing policy is trusted (\cref{sec:q6}).

\section{Experimental Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Trace and datasets}
\label{sec:data}

We use the Alibaba Serverless Infrastructure (ASI) trace released with OSDI~'26~\citep{li2026asi,alibaba2026trace}, which covers six months and up to 155{,}410 GPUs. Scope is online-inference recommendation and GenAI pods with a GPU request. D1 (right-sizing) holds 294{,}465 whole-GPU pods observed for at least 24 hours. D2 (placement) holds 2{,}826{,}036 pods with a readiness label and a 46\% failure rate. Between 61\% and 68\% of pods have no workload history. Days 14--129 are used for training, 130--157 for validation and calibration, and 158--183 for testing. For each of the 10 clusters with at least 9{,}000 pods, models are trained on the other clusters' training days and scored on the held-out cluster's test days.

\subsection{Models and policies}
\label{sec:models}

The baseline \textbf{A1} is the per-workload 90th percentile of past need over the last 7 days, using only pods finished before admission, with a model-type $\times$ GPU-type fallback. \textbf{A2} is regularized linear quantile regression and \textbf{A3} is XGBoost quantile regression with monotone constraints on history features, both at $\tau = 0.90$. Each family received the same budget of three configurations chosen on validation data only. For placement risk, \textbf{B1}, \textbf{B2} and \textbf{B3} are logistic regression, random forest and LightGBM classifiers of failure to become ready. The recovery policy restores a pod's full request after $k$ consecutive short hours. In the simulator, pods keep their original requests; or test pods with an A1 prediction get A1's slice (about 31 of 500 HP pods per snapshot); or every HP online-inference GPU pod gets its average need in that hour, snapped up to the slice grid. The last is an \emph{hourly-need ceiling}, not a deployable policy: it uses the hour's own utilization, and averages rather than peaks. Each simulator variant is also run with the pod's CPU request scaled by the same fraction (\emph{CPU co-sizing}).

\subsection{Configuration}
\label{sec:config}

\paragraph{In-place capacity replay.} We replayed each hour of days 158--184 with pods fixed on their actual servers, across 37{,}832 servers in the 10 unseen clusters. Test pods were shrunk to their predicted slices and all other pods kept their requests. A freed slice counts as usable only if it completes a whole free GPU on that server and the server has enough free CPU for a new job needing the cluster's median CPU per GPU.

\paragraph{Simulator.} Before using ASI data, we confirmed that our build of the FGD simulator reproduces the authors' published allocation results exactly for every deterministic policy. We raised the simulator's limit of 8 GPUs per node to 16, because ASI has 16-GPU servers, and the patched build still matches. We sampled three ASWs with at least 8 servers per cluster and 42 hours from days 158--183: 24 random hours plus two from each cluster's top decile of GPU allocation. This gives 1{,}260 snapshots averaging 46 servers, 427 GPUs and 500 placed pods. With HP pods alone, fewer than 0.5\% of servers exceed their CPU or GPU capacity, while adding preemptible low-priority and best-effort pods puts 46\% of servers over CPU capacity. FGD is run on all snapshots with one seed; the six policies are compared on 240 random-hour snapshots with three seeds each. Intervals are 95\% bootstraps that resample clusters.

\subsection{Impact of cluster shift}
\label{sec:q2}

\finding{The learned model's edge disappears on new clusters.} A3's pinball loss rises from 0.0237 on seen clusters to 0.0259 on unseen clusters, while A1 is unchanged (0.0249 and 0.0247). In three rolling temporal windows on seen clusters at matched risk, A3 reclaims 20.5--23.6\% against 13.9--17.4\% for A1. The learned model's disadvantage is a transfer problem, not a modelling failure.

\finding{Coverage does not transfer, and recalibration does not repair it.} A3's coverage is 92.2\% on unseen clusters against a 95\% target, and its worst single-cluster $U$ reaches 7.3\%, compared with 1.9\% for A1 in the same re-run. Per-cluster recalibration raises reclaim by 3.7--5.6 points but leaves coverage at about 93\%. It mainly loosens margins that were too conservative.

Failure rates range from 35\% to 67\% by cluster, so a model trained elsewhere cannot know a new cluster's base rate. New features improved seen clusters (ROC-AUC 0.79, ECE 0.030) but not unseen ones significantly.

\subsection{Hourly safety and the fallback}
\label{sec:q4}

$U$ scores a pod once. \finding{Reclaimable is not reusable.} Only 3.8\% of freed GPU-hours are usable, against a threshold of 50\% set before running (\cref{tab:chain}). The hypothesis fails even when CPU is ignored (30\%). Without GPU sharing, usable capacity falls to 1.2\%.

\finding{Two limits act together.} The first is \emph{fractional residue}: a pod cut from 1.0 to 0.5 GPU frees half a GPU, and only about 30\% of freed slices add up to whole free GPUs on the same server. The second is \emph{insufficient CPU}, the condition FGD calls ``stranded''~\citep{weng2023fgd}: shrinking a pod releases GPU but not CPU. Clusters \texttt{da8d} and \texttt{4d6f} produced 39\% of freed GPU-hours but only about 6\% of the usable gain, because only 11--17\% of their free whole GPUs had enough CPU even before shrinking. This agrees with ASI's report that CPU shortage is a leading cause of unallocatable GPUs~\citep{li2026asi}.

\finding{The replay isolates what placement allows today.} Pods stay on their servers, only test pods are shrunk (freed GPU-hours are 1.2\% of capacity), and a new job needs the cluster's median CPU. The 3.8\% therefore measures reuse under current placement, with every pod, including preemptible and best-effort pods, counted as holding its capacity. Fleet-wide shrinking, CPU co-sizing and repacking are the levers that can raise it, and \cref{sec:q6,sec:q7,sec:cpu} test them in a simulator.

\finding{The trace cannot validate placement risk for shrunk pods.} Shrinking pods and re-scoring them with Model B reclaims 11.4\% of long-running pods' GPU-hours, but restricted to slice sizes Model B has seen, reclaim collapses to 1.8\%. The trace barely contains the fractional allocations a right-sizing policy would create, so the joint policy is a what-if.

\section{Impact of CPU Co-Sizing}
\label{sec:cpu}

The in-place replay and the simulator point to the same constraint: a freed GPU is reusable only if CPU is free beside it.

\finding{Without CPU co-sizing, much of the freed GPU is stranded.} In the simulator, the hourly-need ceiling frees 15.7 points of whole GPUs, but only 9.5 points are usable with 8 cores each (\cref{tab:emulator}). Scaling each pod's CPU by its GPU fraction makes almost all of it usable ($+18.3$ points). In place, clusters whose free GPUs most often lacked CPU turned 39\% of the freed GPU-hours into about 6\% of the usable gain (\cref{sec:q5}).

\finding{CPU is over-reserved even more than GPU (preliminary).} The ASI pod table records each pod's average CPU use as a share of its request. In one test hour (day 160, hour 12), high-priority online-inference GPU pods (74{,}744) used a median of 9\% to 33\% of their requested CPU depending on model type: 14\% for recommendation pods, which request 16 cores per GPU, and 11\% for GenAI pods.

\finding{Scaling CPU with the GPU slice is the wrong model, though the right direction.} CPU use is only weakly related to GPU need (Spearman $\rho = 0.14$ in the same hour). For 27.5\% of pods, the share of CPU used exceeds the share of GPU needed, so proportional scaling would cut their CPU below use. GenAI and embedding pods need nearly a whole GPU but use about a tenth of their CPU, so proportional scaling leaves most of their spare CPU untouched. The $+18.3$ points should therefore be read as an estimate under a simple rule, not a bound; sizing CPU from each pod's measured use, with headroom for bursts, is the next experiment. This matches prior work showing that CPU sensitivity varies by job~\citep{mohan2022synergy,zhao2020coda}, and that recommendation serving requests far more CPU per GPU than training nodes provide~\citep{yang2025prism}.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Right-sizing touches five bodies of work, and each covers one link in the chain from a prediction to capacity another job can use. Characterization studies show that GPUs are over-reserved. Prediction work shows that past usage forecasts future need. Sharing and placement work shows how to fit more work onto a device or a cluster. CPU-aware scheduling and harvesting show that resources other than the GPU decide what can be reused. Evaluation methodology shows how learned components fail under shift. Our thesis is that no line of work follows a right-sizing decision across all of these links, and that the missing link, whether freed GPU capacity is reusable, is where the savings are decided. We review each theme in turn, drawing on peer-reviewed systems and machine-learning venues from 2011 to 2026; the few preprints we cite are marked as such. \Cref{tab:gaps} summarizes what each line of work leaves open.

\subsection{Evidence of over-reservation}
\label{sec:rw-char}

Every major trace study finds that GPUs are allocated far more than they are used, and the gap has grown as inference has displaced training. Microsoft's Philly cluster allocated most of its GPUs, yet in-use GPUs averaged only about 52\% utilization and 47.7\% of in-use GPU cycles were wasted~\citep{jeon2019philly}. SenseTime's Helios reported cluster utilization of 65--90\% by allocation, with single-GPU jobs forming over half of all jobs but under 6\% of utilization~\citep{hu2021helios}. Alibaba's PAI study is the first to separate request from use for fractional and inference workloads: the median instance requested 0.5 GPU and used 0.042~\citep{weng2022mlaas}. ASI, the trace we use, finds a median SM utilization of only 6\% for online inference across 155{,}410 GPUs~\citep{li2026asi}, and Alibaba's Aegaeon reports that 17.7\% of about 30{,}000 serving GPUs were reserved for models receiving 1.35\% of requests~\citep{xiang2025aegaeon}. Peak provisioning explains much of this: Azure's LLM services see peak-to-valley load ratios of up to 34.6$\times$, and deployments sized for the peak sit underused for the rest of the day~\citep{stojkovic2025dynamollm}. Training clusters are the exception. Acme's LLM development clusters run at median GPU utilization of 97--99\%~\citep{hu2024acme}, so the waste concentrates in inference, which is where we look.

These numbers should not be compared directly, because the studies measure ``utilization'' in three ways: as the share of GPUs allocated (Helios), as the share of time a kernel runs (Philly), and as SM activity (ASI). A GPU can be fully allocated, busy by the first two measures and nearly idle by the third, as memory-bound LLM decoding shows~\citep{patel2024splitwise}. Two studies go further and point at the CPU. PAI found that workloads on 8-GPU machines demanded 1.9$\times$ more CPUs per GPU than the machines provided~\citep{weng2022mlaas}, and ASI attributes idle but unallocatable GPUs mainly to insufficient CPU for job shapes with many cores per GPU~\citep{li2026asi}. Each of these studies covers a single operator, and all of them stop at describing the waste or reclaiming it whole: none asks how much of a per-pod saving would become capacity another job can use.

\subsection{Predicting and right-sizing resources}
\label{sec:rw-pred}

The strongest result in resource prediction is that history works. Resource Central found that a VM's past behavior is its best predictor, reaching 79--90\% accuracy on bucketed metrics~\citep{cortez2017resource}. Google's Autopilot sets limits from each job's usage history and runs jobs with 23\% slack instead of the 46\% that users choose themselves, with ten times fewer out-of-memory events~\citep{rzadca2020autopilot}. The open-source Kubernetes Vertical Pod Autoscaler makes the same choice explicit: its default target is the 90th percentile of past usage plus a 15\% margin, raised by 20\% after an out-of-memory kill~\citep{k8svpa}. Our A1 rule and its fallback are, in effect, this recipe applied to GPU slices.

Learned approaches claim more, but mostly on their own data. FIRM cuts requested CPU limits by up to 62\% with reinforcement learning~\citep{qiu2020firm}, and Sinan saves 25.9--59.0\% of CPU using a violation classifier that is over 94\% accurate~\citep{zhang2021sinan}. Both are trained and tested on the same microservice deployments. MagicScaler, from the same company as our trace, cuts prediction error by 64.3\% and pairs it with a risk constraint, yet its deterministic variant fails on one of its three clusters~\citep{pan2023magicscaler}: the only result we found that hints at the cluster dependence we measure. Inference-serving systems size at the level of instances or model variants rather than pods. MArk and INFaaS reduce serving cost by up to 7.8$\times$ and by 8.5$\times$ on average~\citep{zhang2019mark,romero2021infaas}, Cocktail by 1.45$\times$~\citep{gunasekaran2022cocktail}, and Clipper tunes batching rather than allocation~\citep{crankshaw2017clipper}. Work that sizes GPU shares directly relies on profiling or on model graphs. MISO profiles each job to choose a MIG slice and cuts job completion time by 49\%~\citep{li2022miso}. A MIG-serving study (a preprint) reports 40\% fewer GPUs by mixing slice sizes~\citep{tan2021migserving}, PARIS and ELSA partition reconfigurable GPUs for inference~\citep{kim2022paris}, and iGniter provisions GPU shares under interference to cut cost by up to 25\%~\citep{xu2022igniter}. White-box estimators such as DNNMem and Horus need the model's graph~\citep{gao2020dnnmem,yeung2022horus}, which a trace does not contain, and a recent preprint finds that such estimators generalize poorly~\citep{yousefzadeh2026gpuestimation}.

Taken together, this literature agrees that history is predictive and that the safety rule (a percentile, a margin, a chance constraint, a violation check) shapes outcomes as much as the predictor does. Its weakness is evaluation. Systems are tested inside the fleet or testbed they were built on, and success is reported as lower allocation, slack or cost. None of the work we found trains on some clusters and tests on another, and none asks whether the freed capacity can be used by other work. Our Shift Test and Reuse Ladder address exactly these two omissions.

\subsection{GPU sharing, fragmentation and placement}
\label{sec:rw-share}

A second line of work accepts each request and asks how to fit more of them onto the hardware. At the device level, sharing systems show how much of a GPU small jobs leave idle. Gandiva time-slices and packs training jobs to raise cluster utilization by 26\%~\citep{xiao2018gandiva}. Salus raises inference GPU utilization 42-fold over no sharing~\citep{yu2020salus}, AntMan raises memory and compute utilization by 42\% and 34\% in production~\citep{xiao2020antman}, and REEF lets best-effort inference share a GPU with real-time work at under 2\% added latency~\citep{han2022reef}. Orion, USHER and MuxServe extend sharing to operator-level scheduling, interference-aware placement and multi-LLM serving~\citep{strati2024orion,shubha2024usher,duan2024muxserve}. Aegaeon pools many LLMs per GPU and cut one deployment from 1{,}192 to 213 GPUs~\citep{xiang2025aegaeon}. Hardware partitioning has limits of its own: MIG divides an A100 or H100 into seven compute slices and eight memory slices, so a freed fraction is reusable only if it matches an available profile~\citep{nvidia2026mig}.

At the cluster level, placement heuristics decide where requests land. Tetris introduced multi-resource packing by the dot product of demand and free capacity~\citep{grandl2014tetris}, the origin of the DotProd policy we test. Gavel adapts policies to heterogeneous accelerators and improves job completion time by up to 3.5$\times$~\citep{narayanan2020gavel}. FGD defines a statistical measure of GPU fragmentation and reduces unallocated GPUs by up to 49\% on an emulated cluster of over 6{,}200 GPUs~\citep{weng2023fgd}. These gains, however, were measured on workloads where fractional requests are common. ASI reports that fractional fragmentation has since become negligible because GPU sharing is rarely used~\citep{li2026asi}. Right-sizing would bring fractional requests back, and our simulator shows what happens then: once requests are fixed, five placement policies differ by under 0.6 points of usable capacity, while changing the requests moves it by 8--18 points. All of this work takes the request as given. It improves where a pod goes, not how large the pod should be.

\subsection{CPU as a co-resource, and harvesting idle capacity}
\label{sec:rw-cpu}

The idea that resources should not be tied in fixed proportions is old. Dominant Resource Fairness showed that fixed slots of CPU and memory either under- or over-subscribe machines~\citep{ghodsi2011drf}. For deep learning, Synergy showed that allocating CPU and memory in proportion to GPUs wastes them, and that allocating by measured sensitivity improves job completion time by up to 3.4$\times$~\citep{mohan2022synergy}. CODA sizes CPU cores for training jobs on shared GPU nodes and raises GPU utilization by 20.8\%~\citep{zhao2020coda}, and Muri interleaves training jobs across CPU, storage and network to improve completion time by up to 3.6$\times$~\citep{zhao2022muri}. All three target training, where iterations can be profiled and interleaved; serving pods allow neither. On the serving side, Prism found that recommendation models exhaust CPU and memory before GPUs and fixed this by moving CPU work to separate nodes over RDMA, cutting CPU fragmentation by 53\% and GPU fragmentation by 27\%~\citep{yang2025prism}. A preprint on MIG inference servers finds that slicing a GPU shifts the bottleneck to host-side preprocessing~\citep{yeo2024preba}. Both confirm that CPU binds, but both answer with new hardware or architecture rather than with request sizing.

Harvesting reclaims reserved but idle capacity for lower-priority work. Azure's Harvest VMs grow and shrink with spare cores and deliver the same compute at 91\% lower cost~\citep{ambati2020harvest}. Microsoft's Singularity treats idle reservation as the main waste and reclaims it by preempting and migrating jobs~\citep{shukla2022singularity}. ASI's SpotGPU lends idle high-priority reservations to preemptible jobs, harvests 90\% of released hours, and raises the allocation ratio from 68\% to 93\%~\citep{li2026asi}. ConServe, a preprint, harvests idle GPU time behind online LLM serving~\citep{qiao2025conserve}. Harvesting is effective, but it works at job granularity, it measures success by allocation ratio, and it assumes freed capacity is fungible, as cores are. A freed fraction of a GPU is not fungible: it needs a matching job shape and CPU on the same server. This is why our in-place replay reports 3.8\% when preemptible pods count as occupants, and why \cref{sec:cpu} finds CPU co-sizing to be the lever.

\subsection{Evaluating learned components under shift}
\label{sec:rw-eval}

Our protocol borrows from four strands of methodology. DomainBed showed that, under careful and equal tuning, no domain-generalization method beat empirical risk minimization by more than about a point, and argued that a method without a model-selection rule is incomplete~\citep{gulrajani2021search}. WILDS made held-out natural domains, such as hospitals or regions, the standard test of robustness~\citep{koh2021wilds}. We treat clusters as such domains and safety margins as the selection rule. Conformal prediction supplies the margins~\citep{romano2019cqr,angelopoulos2021gentle}, and its theory predicts how they degrade. Coverage depends on exchangeability; weighting restores it under covariate shift when density ratios are known~\citep{tibshirani2019covariate}; the coverage lost under other shifts is bounded by how far the data depart from exchangeability~\citep{barber2023beyond}; and online adaptation recovers long-run coverage once feedback arrives~\citep{gibbs2021adaptive}. Our coverage fell from a 95\% target to 92\% on unseen clusters, consistent with a bounded loss, and the last two methods are natural next steps.

Leakage and evaluation pitfalls form the third strand. Kapoor and Narayanan found leakage in 329 papers across 17 fields, and in one case study complex models stopped outperforming a decades-old logistic regression once the leak was removed~\citep{kapoor2023leakage}, a pattern our Leak Check repeats for XGBoost against a history rule. TESSERACT showed that time-inconsistent splits inflate malware-detection results~\citep{pendlebury2019tesseract}, and Arp et al. found data snooping in 73\% of 30 top security papers~\citep{arp2022dos}. Pre-registration, our fourth strand, has been tried in machine learning only as a workshop format~\citep{bertinetto2020prereg}; we found no systems precedent. Finally, simulators must be validated before their results are trusted. Gavel checked its simulator against a 48-GPU cluster to within 8\%~\citep{narayanan2020gavel}, and Puffer's randomized trial showed that learned controllers that looked strong in simulation lost to a simple buffer-based rule in deployment~\citep{yan2020puffer}. The shared weakness is scope: shift-aware evaluation scores predictions rather than the decisions made with them, and pitfall surveys cover security and science but not resource management.

Read together, the literature shows a consistent pattern and a consistent gap. GPUs are over-reserved, history predicts need, devices and clusters can be packed more tightly, and the CPU often decides what fits. But each line stops at its own link. Characterization studies stop at describing waste. Prediction work stops at reduced allocation and is rarely tested beyond the fleet it was built on. Placement work assumes requests are correct, and CPU-aware work targets training. Methodology work scores predictions, not decisions. Our two questions sit across these links: whether a learned sizing model helps on a cluster it has never seen, and what a prediction does once it becomes a scheduling decision. R2R answers both on one production trace, and the answer points to the link the literature has studied least: sizing CPU together with GPU at the level of each serving pod.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:conclusion}

On clusters it has not seen, a learned right-sizing model fails to beat a per-workload history rule, and the safety margin moves savings about five times more than the model does. A fifth of reserved GPU-hours is over-reserved at a 5\% risk budget, and a six-hour fallback removes most of the remaining hourly risk. The decisive result is at the end of the Reuse Ladder: only 3.8\% of freed GPU-hours can serve another job under today's placement, when preemptible pods count as occupants. A scheduler simulator shows that how pods are sized, and whether their CPU shrinks with them, matters far more than which placement policy repacks them. More investment in GPU prediction targets the wrong constraint. The path to real savings is to size GPU and CPU together.

\paragraph{Using R2R as an operator.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Before trusting a sizing model on a new cluster, run the Shift Test against a history rule. On ASI the rule was as good and needs no training.
  \item Choose the safety margin deliberately: it moves savings five times more than the model.
  \item Deploy with a fallback that restores the full request after six consecutive short hours.
  \item Count savings at the top of the Reuse Ladder, as whole GPUs with CPU beside them, not as reclaimed GPU-hours.
  \item Size CPU together with GPU; the placement policy is a secondary choice.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Replication.} DomainBed repeats its full sweeps three times with new random choices, and WILDS uses at least three replicates. We report one pipeline run per configuration, with paired tests across clusters.
  \item \textbf{ID comparison is not test-to-test.} WILDS recommends measuring in-distribution performance on the test distribution. Our seen-cluster results use future days across clusters rather than the 10 held-out clusters themselves.
  \item \textbf{No latency data.} $U$ and short pod-hours are proxies, and the trace has no request-latency field. Usage was recorded with the full GPU available, so a shortfall's true effect is not observed.
  \item \textbf{Hourly resolution and P90 replays.} Sub-hour bursts are invisible, and the replays used slices built on the P90 label, so they are probably slightly optimistic for a memory-peak-safe policy.
  \item \textbf{Test days have been reused.} H5--H10 are exploratory, and H11 and H12 had rules set before running on days already used. No later days remain for a fresh holdout.
  \item \textbf{Simulator scope.} The simulator models CPU and GPU but not memory, places static snapshots without arrivals or departures, and keeps pods within their ASW. The hourly-need ceiling uses hourly averages. Random placement is not bit-for-bit reproducible, because the simulator draws random numbers during parallel scoring.
  \item \textbf{One operator.} All results come from one six-month trace with 10 usable held-out clusters.
\end{itemize}

\section{Preliminary: Incumbent Scheduler Rules}
\label{app:schedulers}

An unmerged analysis replays sizing rules recovered from the release binaries of six open-source GPU schedulers (VPA, kube-scheduler, KAI, HAMi, Volcano, Koordinator) on 22 days of the trace. All six size GPUs from what the user declares, so the deployed incumbent reclaims nothing, and a fixed factor such as 85\% snaps back to a whole GPU on the slice grid. On clusters seen in training, 90--95\% of each policy's reclaim survives cluster-level packing when server and CPU limits are ignored. Together with \cref{sec:q5}, this suggests that server and CPU limits, not packing itself, account for much of the loss. These numbers are preliminary: the window is short, pods still running at its end have truncated labels, the recovered constants have not been cross-checked against source, and VPA has not been evaluated on unseen clusters. We therefore do not report its model comparisons here.
